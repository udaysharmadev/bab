Phase Zero Preproduction Report — Four
Players, One Tiny Boat, One Enormous Journey
Executive summary, assumptions and scope
This report treats the uploaded master brief as the authoritative product mandate: a real-time 3D
browser game built around exactly four human paddlers sharing one small wooden boat, with
locomotion arising from four independently controlled paddles rather than ordinary vehicle
controls. It also treats the requested phase lifecycle, evidence discipline, permanent documentation,
continuous-world ambition, multiplayer authority model and “prove the paddling before building the
world” rule as binding constraints. fileciteturn0file0
The strongest direction is not “a four-player Paddle Paddle Paddle”. Nor should it become “Fart
Together , except with oars”. Paddle Paddle Paddle already centres a physics-paddling obstacle journey,
while Fart Together explicitly supports 2–4 people applying force, direction and timing to the same raft.
Those similarities make superficial differentiation unusually dangerous. 
The defensible mechanical identity is:
Four spatially distributed physical blades interacting with a shared, moving body of
water.
That distinction sounds small, but it changes the whole game. A paddle can miss the water , enter too
shallowly, catch a reverse current, scrape a wall, become a temporary brace, apply force at the bow
rather than stern, or create yaw because another crew member stroked late. The boat's behaviour
becomes the understandable consequence of where four people applied force, when they applied it,
how the water itself was moving, and where the hull was struck. Rapier directly supports
application of forces and impulses at world points, which is exactly the mechanism needed to translate
blade forces into both linear acceleration and torque rather than faking steering. 
The second major differentiator should be the expedition structure. Paddle Paddle Paddle markets
one giant handcrafted level with configurable checkpoints; Chained Together demonstrates the appeal
of an arduous shared ascent and separate checkpoint/difficulty modes; RV There Yet? demonstrates the
social legibility of four players depending on one physical vehicle; PEAK demonstrates that replayable
conditions can refresh co-op without replacing the core mechanic.  The proposed game therefore
uses one coherent continent-scale hydrological journey—The Grand Waterworks—rather than
unrelated obstacle arenas.
The third differentiator is recovery before failure. The memorable outcome should often be “we
somehow saved that” rather than “touch obstacle, reload checkpoint”. This is a design hypothesis to
validate, not a proven recipe for virality. Research on co-operative play supports treating
communication and coordination as first-class aspects of multiplayer teamwork, while physics-oriented
co-op games repeatedly build their public positioning around shared mistakes, dependence and
recovery; however , neither review percentages nor creator attention prove that any single mechanic
caused commercial success.

The recommended working title is All Hands, No Rudder. It communicates the four-person crew, the
absence of conventional steering and the comic danger in one phrase. It is not legally cleared. An
initial exact-title search did not surface a competing game with that complete title, but “ALL HANDS”
exists in unrelated live marks, including entertainment-related usage, so professional clearance remains
mandatory before announcement. 
The recommended technical baseline is TypeScript + Three.js + Rapier 3D + Colyseus + Node.js +
PostgreSQL, with WebSocket transport at launch, a robust WebGL2 rendering baseline and WebGPU
enhancements only after parity testing. Three.js can target WebGPU and fall back to WebGL2, while
PlayCanvas similarly exposes a dual WebGPU/WebGL2 strategy; current browser-engine documentation
still makes a fallback path the prudent choice. 
The authoritative simulation should run at a fixed timestep. The first buoyancy implementation should
use distributed hull samples rather than CFD. Paddle forces should be calculated from blade
submersion and water-relative velocity, applied at the relevant paddle station. Visual water and
gameplay water should share a deterministic low-frequency sampler while GPU-only waves, foam,
reflection and refraction add visual fidelity. Research on real-time buoyancy and rigid-body/water
interaction shows that sophisticated two-way water simulation exists, but also reinforces why games
routinely need cheaper real-time approximations rather than a full fluid solver . 
The highest-risk problem is not rendering. It is the combination of shared-body physics and
networking. Four clients are simultaneously manipulating one authoritative rigid body. Colyseus now
provides explicit facilities for fixed-step prediction, reconciliation, interpolation, reconnection and state
synchronisation, but those facilities do not make a shared Rapier world automatically pleasant under
100–200 ms latency. That has to be experimentally proven. 
Accordingly, the project should have one uncompromising production gate:
Do not author the enormous world until four humans can paddle a tiny test boat around a dull
test basin and immediately want another run.
A filesystem-backed Phase Zero documentation pack has been created from this research. It contains
the requested PRD, research log, phase tracker , decision log, world atlas, physics specification,
multiplayer architecture, art bible, test matrix, risk register , changelog, name research, implementation
plan, first-prototype specification and obstacle catalogue. It explicitly records no implementation,
playtest, benchmark or deployment as complete.

Scope assumptions. The target audience, budget and team size were unspecified, so this report
assumes a serious small independent studio rather than a solo weekend project or AAA headcount.
Desktop browsers are the primary launch target; mobile is not a launch constraint. The intended
flagship experience always uses four humans, although a later practice mode for fewer players is
compatible with the brief if it never becomes the design centre. Exact commercial price, schedule,
staffing and server cost cannot honestly be fixed before the Phase One and Phase Two benchmarks.
The report therefore separates evidence, design recommendation, prototype target and unverified
hypothesis throughout.

Review numbers are useful for establishing that several of these games attracted substantial audiences,
but not for identifying causal success factors. As of this research pass, Steam shows roughly 1,609
reviews at 86% positive for Paddle Paddle Paddle, 20,658 English reviews at 90% for Chained Together ,
32,026 English reviews at 89% for RV There Yet?, and 146,863 English reviews at 95% for PEAK. Those are
time-sensitive storefront snapshots, not experimental evidence that “physics”, “rage” or any particular
checkpoint policy caused demand. 
A particularly useful negative lesson comes from Paddle Paddle Paddle's own post-launch changes: the
developer identified camera controls as a significant problem for players and changed the default
camera behaviour , while also revising a confusing pipe puzzle.  That is a direct warning against
treating the camera as presentation rather than control infrastructure. In this project, a missed gate
caused by the crew should be funny; a missed gate caused by an unreadable camera will feel defective.

The exact originality line should be explicit.
Paddle Paddle Paddle asks, essentially, “can one or two players manipulate paddles through an obstacle
journey?”  Fart Together asks, “can 2–4 players coordinate thrust-like force, direction and timing on
one raft?” All Hands, No Rudder should ask, “can a four-person crew learn to operate one
physically coherent boat when every blade, current, collision and mistake changes the same rigid
body?”
That requires five non-copyable-feeling systems working together:
Distributed force points. The bow-port paddler and stern-port paddler are not interchangeable
because their force vectors act at different lever arms around the hull.
Water-relative strokes. The same gesture produces a different result in still water , a cross-
current, an eddy or a backward-moving lock chamber .
Meaningful misses and braces. A paddle outside the water cannot secretly propel the hull. A
blade planted against a wall can physically brace or pry, within bounded energy limits.
Recoverable instability. Players can rescue a bad yaw, scrape along a wall, counter a roll or
back out of a wedge rather than immediately lose.

The resulting creative direction is storybook expedition realism. The world should have stylised,
instantly readable forms rather than photorealism, but its wood, stone, ice, metal, vegetation and water
should feel tactile and physically grounded. The tiny warm-timber boat is always the focal point against
cooler rivers, reservoirs and monumental civil engineering. Giant infrastructure, changing weather and
deep atmospheric perspective create scale; saturation and bloom do not.
The fantasy is not “four idiots in an obstacle game”. It is:
Four friends become, slowly and painfully, a real crew.
At the beginning, they overcorrect every bend. Halfway through, they begin calling cadence without
being prompted. Late in the game, one player sees a dangerous cross-current and the crew instinctively
changes stroke before anyone finishes explaining it. The reward is not a +20% paddle. The progression
is the humans learning each other.
That is the most important creative opportunity in the project.

Recommended control language: direct blade workspace. Each player's paddle has a constrained
ergonomic region. Pointer or analogue-stick movement moves the blade target through that region at
bounded physical speed. Holding the primary action immerses the blade; moving while immersed
creates a stroke; release raises/feathers it. Pushing forward while immersed creates a reverse stroke.
The system maps player intent to paddle pose, never to boat thrust.
A player therefore learns three verbs: dip, pull, release.
Mouse users move the pointer relative to a captured local paddle space; the primary mouse button
plants the blade. Controller users use the right stick for blade position and the trigger for immersion. A
keyboard fallback uses four direction keys plus a plant key to move through the same constrained
paddle state. Camera input is separate and cannot apply steering force.
The launch boat should use two port and two starboard stations, staggered fore and aft. Each
paddle is attached conceptually to its own player and applies forces at a unique hull-relative point. Four
equal forward strokes therefore yield approximately straight acceleration; port-only strokes yaw one
direction; starboard-only strokes the opposite; opposing forward/reverse strokes can pivot; a bow
stroke and stern stroke of equal magnitude create different rotational responses because their moment
arms differ .
Manual leaning should not be part of the first prototype. The body animation can lean naturally with
stroke forces and boat acceleration, but a second independent balance axis risks obscuring the one-
control-per-person elegance. A Phase Three experiment may test modest weight transfer only after
paddling alone is deep enough.
The authoritative boat is one Rapier dynamic rigid body with explicit mass, inertia and centre-of-mass
tuning. Rapier supports force and impulse application at points, so distributed buoyancy and paddle
reactions can naturally generate translational and rotational movement rather than manually setting
yaw.

supports an intentionally bounded gameplay model instead of a fluid solver .

The water system should deliberately split authoritative physics water from visual water. The
authoritative sampler outputs low-frequency height, surface normal and current vector
deterministically. The renderer can add much richer normals, reflection, refraction, foam, wake and
micro-waves. Classic real-time water work explicitly demonstrates the effectiveness of combining lower-
frequency geometric wave motion with high-frequency normal detail rather than attempting to model
every part of the water volumetrically. 
Capsizing should be a state, not an instant trigger . A dangerous roll can still be countered by strokes/
bracing/current. Only sustained inversion/submersion or an unrecoverable fall should trigger failure.
Grounding should increase hull contact and drag; wedging should offer a reset only after a short
detector confirms persistent contacts and negligible progress. Small crashes should produce sound,
wood creak, spray, character reactions and changed momentum—not a red “FAIL” overlay.
Checkpoint restoration must be deterministic and boring internally even if activation is spectacular
visually. The server loads a canonical safe boat pose, clears current forces/velocities, places the four
characters into valid seat states, resolves overlaps, advances several controlled simulation steps if
necessary, and only then returns player authority. Persisting arbitrary live velocity from the instant of
checkpoint activation is unnecessary risk.
Adventure checkpoint spacing should begin with a design target of roughly 5–10 minutes of
mastered traversal between meaningful saves, becoming denser around especially lethal set pieces.
That number is a hypothesis for testing. Challenge can space them farther apart. Time Trial should use
the same course but distinguish valid competitive runs from heavily assisted/resumed runs. Chained
Together's separation of checkpoint-assisted Beginner from leaderboard-meaningful harder modes
provides a useful structural precedent without dictating the exact cadence. 
The camera is a gameplay system. It should use a third-person trailing anchor with velocity-based look-
ahead, mild route-aware anticipation, obstacle sphere casts and hand-authored tunnel/canyon camera
volumes. The camera should communicate boat roll without inheriting the hull's horizon rotation 1:1.
FOV may widen gently with speed, while high-speed waterfalls prioritise look-ahead over cinematic
shake. Local free-look should auto-recentre. The importance of treating this as a core system is
strengthened by Paddle Paddle Paddle's documented post-launch camera revision. 
Animation follows physical state. Hands use IK against the actual paddle handle; shoulder and torso
poses react to blade resistance; characters lean from the boat's angular/linear acceleration; a missed
water stroke visibly “air-swings”; collision reactions use actual acceleration/contact magnitude.
Animation never decides propulsion.
Sound is similarly informational: blade entry, clean catch, cavitating/sloppy stroke, wood scrape, hard
impact, hull creak, water rushing faster beside the boat, paddle-on-rock contact and current intensity
should all help four people infer what happened without examining an HUD.
The HUD should contain almost nothing during ordinary play: seat colour/name, reconnect information
when necessary, subtle checkpoint confirmation and optional route/progress information. No steering
gauges, stamina bars, paddle-power meters or RPG inventory are required.

The visual direction should exploit browser-friendly composition rather than brute force. Repeated
environmental elements should use instancing or batching; Three.js explicitly documents draw-call
reduction through merged geometry and InstancedMesh.  Assets should use glTF 2.0 with
Meshopt/Draco where appropriate and KTX2/Basis textures; Three.js's loader supports those
compression paths, and browser-engine documentation emphasises that apparently small JPEG/PNG
downloads expand substantially when uncompressed in VRAM. 
The game's art hierarchy should therefore be: exquisite boat and paddle close-up quality; strong
characters; highly authored hero landmarks; medium-detail traversable surroundings; aggressively
instanced/supporting vegetation and infrastructure; distant proxies. The tiny boat should always look
like the most tangible object in the world.
World Atlas, route logic and obstacle system
The world concept is The Grand Waterworks: a natural watershed that generations of civilisation have
progressively dammed, diverted, lifted, channelled, tunnelled and industrialised. That gives the game a
coherent reason to move from wild river to flooded city to dam to subterranean turbines to locks to
pumped highland channels to glacial aqueducts and finally a gigantic cascade to the ocean.
Vertical ascent is never explained by the boat magically climbing a dry wall. Locks, boat lifts,
counterweighted troughs, pumped flumes and moving water platforms perform the work. The high-
altitude sections can become increasingly fantastical, but the world first teaches a consistent “water
infrastructure can move us uphill” grammar .
These elevations and durations are fictional design targets, not engineering survey data or promised
playtime.

Every region must also pass a production checklist: continuous entrance/exit geography; at least one
recognisable distant landmark; a unique acoustic layer; one major new hazard and several returning
hazards; a recovery route; checkpoint validation; streaming manifest; collision complexity review; high/
medium/low rendering profile; four-client network test; camera-volume pass; and first-time readability

A difficulty grammar should prevent “hard” from meaning “more random things”. Each obstacle has
independent dimensions: approach speed, prediction horizon, spatial clearance, environmental force,
recovery width and coordination asymmetry. Raise only one or two dimensions when first teaching a
hazard. Combine familiar hazards only after each component has been learned. Randomness may
change exact trajectories but must not erase readable telegraphs.
The strongest obstacle ideas for early development are current seams, eddies, standing waves, shallow
grounding, waterwheel gaps, logs, crosswinds, low ceilings, submerged boulders, spillway chutes, four-
stroke gates, reverse-braking docks, spin alignment and wall bracing. They exercise the core paddle/
boat system without requiring elaborate one-off machinery. Boat elevators, moving troughs, geometric
hydraulic jumps, dynamic ice and deforming nets should wait until the fundamental simulation is
proven.

Recommendation: Three.js, because the game's unusual value lies in simulation/network integration
and custom world streaming more than editor-driven entity authoring. The price is that the team must
build disciplined level/obstacle tooling instead of relying on a full editor ecosystem. A Phase One
PlayCanvas spike should still be run as a falsification test: if its editor/pipeline produces materially faster
iteration without hurting shared simulation architecture, the decision can change.
For rigid-body physics, Rapier is the leading candidate. Its JavaScript/WASM API explicitly supports
forces/impulses at points, and its determinism documentation states that the WASM/JavaScript version
can be deterministic across platforms when version, initial conditions and insertion order remain
consistent, while also warning about sources such as platform-dependent JavaScript trigonometric
functions.  That is unusually helpful for browser/server parity.
Jolt is a credible high-performance alternative and cannon-es is a lightweight JavaScript alternative, but
both create different trade-offs: Jolt adds a C++/WASM integration and build surface, while cannon-es is
intentionally a lightweight JavaScript physics library.  Neither should replace Rapier without a
benchmark demonstrating a concrete advantage for the actual boat/contact workload.
For networking, Colyseus is recommended over raw WebSocket plumbing because it already
supplies authoritative Room abstractions, server-mutated synchronised state, property-level state
changes, reconnection mechanisms, current prediction/reconciliation guidance and explicit load-test/

The proposed topology is:
The server never accepts “apply 4,000 N here” from a client. It receives bounded paddle intent:
sequence number , client tick, local target blade position/velocity and immersion state. It clamps physical
reach and maximum blade speed, reconstructs the server-side blade state, samples local water and
calculates the resulting force. That is both better anti-cheat and a cleaner source of truth.
The initial simulation target should be 60 fixed physics steps per second, benchmarked directly
against a 30 Hz server alternative. Colyseus documentation exposes fixed timestep/prediction patterns
and a default state-patching cadence in this general range, but the actual room tick and replication
cadence are project decisions, not framework guarantees.  A sensible first experiment is 60 Hz
authoritative physics with approximately 20 Hz network snapshots/deltas.

World rendering should stream chunks based on route topology and visibility, not arbitrary equal
squares. The next route branches/chunks are prefetched according to boat position and velocity;
previous chunks remain resident long enough for reversal and recovery. Distant hero landmarks use
proxy meshes that are loaded far earlier than local traversal assets. Dynamic obstacle simulation
activates only around the crew, while static collision and broad route data can remain substantially
cheaper .
glTF is the appropriate asset delivery format; Khronos positions it as a runtime 3D asset-delivery
standard, while Three.js directly supports mesh compression and KTX2/Basis texture extensions. 
Browser memory management needs explicit disposal because Three.js's glTF documentation warns
that image bitmaps are not automatically garbage-collected merely because the loader's object
reference disappears.  This is particularly important in a multi-hour streaming game.

The smallest genuine prototype should contain almost nothing that cannot help answer the core
question.
Prototype environment: a roughly 150 m × 60 m test basin, flat/low-wave water , one wooden
placeholder boat, four clearly marked seats/paddles, 8–12 slalom pylons, one narrow gate, one cross-
current volume and one reset/checkpoint dock. No giant world, cosmetics, progression, elaborate
characters or advanced weather .
Prototype networking: one real Colyseus room, four separate browser clients, authoritative Rapier
boat, fixed simulation tick, input sequencing, authoritative state stream and reconnect test. One client
process pretending to be four players is insufficient for the gate.
Prototype input: direct blade workspace only. Every useful boat movement must ultimately derive from
water/solid interaction with the paddles plus environmental forces. No hidden arrow-key or WASD
velocity motor .

Initial acceptance thresholds should be treated as pre-registered design targets rather than
manipulated after seeing results. A reasonable first gate is that at least 75% of a minimum twelve first-
time testers demonstrate the basic dip/pull/release cycle within 30 seconds; at least 80% rate “I
understood why the boat moved that way” at 4/5 or above after tutorial play; a controlled missed-water
gesture produces no more than a small fraction—target ≤5%—of matched immersed-stroke impulse;
and no uncontrolled force explosion, tunnelling catastrophe or hard desynchronisation occurs during a
30-minute four-player test session. These are proposed acceptance criteria, not current results.
The most revealing qualitative measure should be recorded immediately after every run:
“When the boat did something bad, could you identify which stroke/current/contact
caused it?”
That question differentiates good difficulty from bad controls.
Other metrics should include time-to-first-clean-stroke, number of accidental versus understood
failures, correction magnitude under network reconciliation, number of recoveries from apparently bad
situations, time stuck in wedges, spontaneous verbal coordination, checkpoint repetition, voluntary
immediate replays and the point at which a player first begins calling a cadence or directing another
seat.
The project should not treat “players yelled a lot” as proof of fun.
The first major decision gate after human testing should have three possible outcomes:
Proceed if four-person paddling is understandable, expressive and repeatedly enjoyable.
Iterate if the social idea works but control causality, drag, torque or latency makes the boat unreliable.
Stop/reconcept if players consistently prefer a hidden steering assist or cannot perceive their
individual contribution. Building fourteen regions around a weak shared-control mechanic would only
make the failure more expensive.
